## Deep vs Shallow Knowledge in an AI world
AI is going to change the programming landscape greatly.

I've been a professional developer for 10 years, and programming for about 14. I've been lucky to work with many different companies as a consultant. I came across [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z0SJNkzOiq4&ab_channel=gamedevcuts) a few weeks ago from Jonathan Blow, the lord almighty of hot takes... Though I was initially dismissive, I find some of his points to be relatable. He claims that the developers of today have mostly what he calls shallow knowledge. He defines as shallow knowledge is largely unrelated to the amount of knowledge you have for a certain topic. Rather it's the topic itself that is critical. Programmers today are focused less on fundamental concepts and more on building apps within frameworks. Obviously to some degree he's right, that's the main selling point of coding boot camps.

But id argue whether you studied cs at Stanford, or did a 3 month coding boot camp, the industry forces a cycle of shallow learning on us all.

## It's unavoidable
Over the last decade, the landscape has changed enormously. For reference, in 2014, I was working on mostly Java backend applications, in Java EE or Spring. We were building MVC style web applications, with server side templating, and we were deploying to a shared java application server (tomcat or jboss etc...). I recall having to learn tomcat / jboss administration, in order to make all of our applications run on the shared runtime. I also learned several different templating engines like freemarker, velocity, mustache (jsp had already fallen out of style thank god). I think about just how many days I spent researching technologies that are now completely obsolete. It bums me out.

Now its not all wasted time, there are things I've learned that have had a lasting impact. Writing a lot of backend Java code gives you a really good perspective on some good things like encapsulated service based architecture, and bad things like over abstraction. Being able to contrast the way our applications were structured then vs now gives a lot of insight into what worked well and what we are still trying to figure out. I would describe these things as "Deep" knowledge, as it is relevant well past the application that it came from.

I wish I could say that this was the majority of my time spent learning, but I spent at least as much time learning Frameworks, build tools, CI tools, configuration management etc... Some of these technologies are simply an opinionated take on doing "something", which many developers might have liked at the time. The community opinion changes, when better ideas come along. Not to mention basically every single thing we did in the frontend space is different now. 

## "Deep" knowledge is difficult
In my own experience, I'm in a constant loop of needing to learn just enough about something to make it work in a project. When I get any time away from projects (which is rare), I spend time learning new useful things, mostly catching up on things I didn't have time for. Important concepts that I wasn't exposed to, or lately AWS certificates. The certs are very useful for myself and my company, but there's no denying it's shallow knowledge. It's simply one company's opinion of cloud computing. 

Other industries aren't like this. I have friends and family working in other engineering disciplines, and there is simply no comparison. Engineers routinely spend 2-3 months "training" on new methods, workflows, and technologies. 

## AI will change our industry, but how?
The last 10 years might have changed our industry in important ways, but I feel it was predictable. I think many of the trends were iterative improvements to existing paradigms. The massive industry shakeups that we saw like Cloud computing, IAC, Kubernetes, were big cahnges to our industry, but perhaps not the world changing disrupters that the marketing material may have you believe. As impactful as they were, they were iterations on good ideas, taken to their next step. 
I'll be among the first to admit that I have no idea what the next 10 years will look like. TV and Movie depictions of AI have been like watchiing the Jetsons; a future based on archaic understandings of what matters to people and how things work.
